Part 11Classification — the cost band

The task asked for classification where it adds value. The interface needs one label that a number cannot supply, so that is the target: is this trip cheap or expensive for its length?

11.1 Why the target is ₹/km and not rupees

Absolute cost is close to determined by distance. A 1,200 km drive costs more than a 200 km one without being worse value, so classifying total cost would tell a user nothing they could not read off the headline figure.

cost_band is therefore a quartile split of ₹/km into Excellent / Good / Average / Poor. That isolates the thing the user is actually choosing between: vehicle, mileage and fuel.

71.4% is partly high by construction, and saying so is part of the answer

cost_band is a discretisation of a quantity the Part 8 regressor already predicts to within ₹34. Some of this accuracy is therefore inherited rather than earned, and a report that quoted it without that sentence would be overselling.

It earns its place because the interface needs the label, not because it demonstrates difficult learning. Volunteering this distinction is stronger than being caught by it.

11.3 The second row is the experiment

The two rows above are the same target, the same split and the same model. The only difference is that the second run drops toll_cost and fuel_consumption_litres — the two columns no traveller can supply, which the sub-models in Part 10 predict on their behalf.

So the 6.0-point gap is not a footnote about feature selection. It is a measurement of what those sub-models are worth, in the currency of the thing they feed. Without them the classifier still beats a 25.7% baseline comfortably; with them it does measurably better, and the difference is attributable rather than asserted.

This is also where the overfitting diagnosis finally bites

On the cost target, step 2 of the Task 5 checklist was close to uninformative: every model scored above R² 0.99 because the target is a formula, so train-versus-test told you almost nothing. Here it separates the models immediately.

ModelTrainTestGapVerdict
LogisticRegression0.72790.7142+0.014Good fit
RandomForest (300)1.00000.7160+0.284Overfitting
DecisionTree1.00000.6438+0.356Overfitting

A tree grown to purity memorises every training row and reports perfect training accuracy while losing 28 to 36 points on held-out data.

Note which model has the best raw test score. RandomForest edges LogisticRegression by 0.0018 — well inside the cross-validation spread — while carrying a train-test gap twenty times larger. The selection rule used throughout this project (error band, then the one-standard-error rule, then simplicity) picks the logistic model, and this is exactly the case it exists for: a score that is higher by less than its own uncertainty is not higher.

11.4 How the label earns its place in the product

A classifier that outputs one word is decoration unless the word changes a decision. This one sits on every result card, beside a figure the user is about to compare against two others.

Because the interface prices a hatchback, a sedan and an SUV on the same route, the three bands are read together rather than alone — and they separate cleanly. On Agra → Jaipur at 241 km:

FuelHatchbackSedanSUV
Petrol at ₹106.50Good — ₹2,028Average — ₹2,299Poor — ₹2,930
Diesel at ₹98.20Excellent — ₹1,793Good — ₹2,022Average — ₹2,542

The rupee figures alone would leave the user to work out whether ₹2,930 is bad value or simply a bigger car. The band answers that directly — and the second row shows the same three vehicles each moving up one band on a cheaper fuel, which is the comparison the whole section exists to make.
