Part 6 · Week 4Proving the dataset is synthetic

6.1 The question that started it

Week 3 ended at R² = 0.9928. That is a suspiciously good score for a dataset with weather, traffic and human behaviour in it. So Week 4 asked a different question — not “how well can we predict the target” but “what actually generated it?”

The method is deliberately simple: subtract the cost components we already know about and study what is left. If the residual is structured rather than random, the data came from a formula.

raw['fuel_cost'] = raw.fuel_consumption_litres * raw.fuel_price
raw['residual']  = raw.total_trip_cost - raw.fuel_cost - raw.toll_cost - raw.parking_cost

Two facts jump out. The residual is never negative and never zero — so something is systematically missing, not randomly scattered. And it correlates +0.8462 with distance. Something proportional to distance is unaccounted for.

6.3 Finding which column controls it

Dividing the residual by distance gives a per-kilometre rate. Grouping that rate by each categorical column shows which one governs it:

Only vehicle_type splits it, and it splits it cleanly. That residual is a per-kilometre maintenance / wear-and-tear charge that depends on the vehicle.

6.4 The recovered formula

total_trip_cost = fuel_consumption_litres x fuel_price
                + toll_cost
                + parking_cost
                + distance_km x maintenance_rate(vehicle_type)

maintenance_rate (Rs/km):   Hatchback 0.5694   Sedan 0.6943   SUV 0.8366

fuel_consumption_litres = (distance_km / mileage) x traffic_multiplier
traffic_multiplier:         Low 0.9445   Medium 1.0446   High 1.1653
estimated_time_min/km:      Low 0.919    Medium 1.059    High 1.263
toll = distance x N(1.301, 0.360) Rs/km
parking is drawn uniformly from {0, 40, 60, 80, 120, 150}
weather_condition has NO effect on anything

Where that multiplier goes in the finished product

The recovered formula says fuel burn runs above the textbook distance / mileage, by an amount that depends on driving conditions. That is a real effect and it is worth about 4–17%.

But the interface never asks about conditions, because a traveller cannot state them before setting off — nobody knows what traffic they will meet. So the litres sub-model is given distance and mileage only, and it absorbs the multiplier into a single fitted slope: 1.0497, which sits almost exactly on the mix of conditions in the data. The spread around it becomes irreducible error rather than a feature, and Part 12.4 measures what that costs.

This is the pattern the whole project turns on: an effect being real is not sufficient reason to put it in the form. It also has to be something the user can answer.

What this result means, and why it is the intellectual centre of the project

R² = 0.999162 from arithmetic alone. No model was fitted — the formula was recovered by inspection and then evaluated. Two consequences follow:

  • Week 3's R² = 0.9928 was measuring formula recovery, not predictive skill. The model was rediscovering an equation that generates the data exactly, which is why the score was so high.
  • It also unlocks Week 5: because the generating physics is known, a much larger dataset can be generated over real geography that is physically consistent with the original.

This is also the reason the project reports two accuracy figures. If the target is a formula and the model is handed three of its four terms, a near-perfect score is arithmetic, not intelligence — the point Weeks 9 and 10 pursue to its conclusion.

Part 7 · Week 5Scaling to real geography, and the distance table

7.1 Why this was necessary

The original dataset covers only 9 fixed city pairs, 110–460 km. A model trained on it cannot serve a user who wants Kochi to Nagpur — it has never seen a route it was not given. To build something usable, the dataset had to cover arbitrary Indian routes.

The limitation this creates, stated before being asked

The cost relationships are learned from generated trips. They are physically consistent with the original data — same recovered formula, real coordinates, real geography — but they are not independent evidence, and no amount of volume changes that. 25,000 rows of generated data is 25,000 rows of the same assumption.

One thing does break out of it: the distance side of the product is measured, not generated. Part 12 fits the distance model on 840 real OSRM routes, and the reference table below is built the same way. The limitation is therefore bounded rather than merely admitted — Part 12.2 measures how far the generated convention sits from the real road network (−0.42% in the mean, 6.3% route by route).

7.4 The distance reference table

A user is asked for exactly one thing they have to look up: how far they are driving. So the site ships the answer rather than making them find it elsewhere — a searchable table of measured road distances, built offline and served as a static file.

PropertyValue
Cities139, hand-picked, across 28 states
Pairs fetched9,591
Routes shipped5,538 (50–1,500 km)
File size74.7 KB raw, 25.6 KB gzipped
Build cost6 HTTP requests, about 10 seconds, run once and committed
SourceOSRM /table, driving profile

Why /table and not /route

scripts/fetch_real_distances.py asks OSRM for one route at a time, which is right when the routes are unrelated. Here every city is wanted against every other, and the /table endpoint answers that shape directly: one request returns a full N×N distance matrix.

The limit is exactly 100 coordinates — 101 returns HTTP 400 TooBig — so larger lists are covered by pairing blocks of 50 and slicing the cross sub-matrix out of each response. A 100-coordinate request returns 4,950 pairs in about 1.3 seconds. The same work through the per-pair loop would take hours and would trip rate limiting long before finishing.

The two endpoints were checked against each other on the 34 pairs that appear both in this table and in data/real_distances.csv: mean difference 0.008%, worst 0.131%. They are the same road network, asked a different way.

Why the city list is hand-written, and not the top 139 by population

GeoNames ranks administrative units, not the places people name when they describe a drive. Taking its top rows verbatim puts Rasapudipalem above Visakhapatnam, lists Pimpri twice (once as Pimpri, once as Pimpri-Chinchwad), and fills the table with Mumbai → Dharavi at 8.5 km.

A city autocomplete hides that, because nobody searches for a suburb they have never heard of. A browsable table does not. Every name in the list is therefore written out by hand; the coordinates still come from india_cities.csv, so the names are curated and the geography is not.

A proximity pass runs afterwards as a safety net: walking in population order, any city within 40 km of one already accepted is dropped. It caught 15 satellites the names alone let through — Noida beside Delhi, Bhiwandi beside Mumbai, Howrah beside Kolkata, Bokaro beside Dhanbad.

Why the table is filtered to 50–1,500 km

That is the range the cost model was trained on. Random pairs drawn from a national city list are mostly long-haul, and 4,053 of the 9,591 fetched pairs exceed 1,500 km. An unfiltered table would invite the user to type a number the estimator then has to flag as extrapolation, roughly two times in five.

Those routes are real and they are fetched; they are simply left out of the shipped file. A reference page should not hand the user a number its own calculator distrusts. Anything outside the band, or any pair the table does not carry, goes to /api/distance, which asks OSRM live and falls back to the fitted distance model when routing is unreachable — and says which of the two answered.

Part 8 · Week 6One engineered feature beats two ensembles

8.1 The testable claim

Week 3 named the linear model's structural blind spot:

The real fuel bill is the product litres × price, and a linear model can only add its features together, never multiply them.

That is a claim that can be tested. If the missing product is really the bottleneck, then supplying it as a feature should beat throwing a nonlinear model at the problem — because a tree ensemble has to approximate a product it can never represent exactly, whereas we can simply hand it over.

8.2 The experiment

Four models, same 25,000 rows, same 80/20 split, same six base features (distance_km, mileage, fuel_price, toll_cost, parking_cost, fuel_consumption_litres):

ModelTest R²Test MAE
Linear (no interaction)0.994666₹143.85
RandomForest, 300 trees0.998543₹69.97
GradientBoosting0.998560₹73.50
Linear + litres × price0.999614₹42.60

The prediction holds. One engineered column takes MAE from ₹143.85 to ₹42.60 — a 3.4× improvement — and beats both ensembles, which had access to exactly the same information but had to learn the product implicitly.

8.3 The second missing feature

Week 4 found the per-km maintenance rate splits by vehicle: Hatchback 0.5694, Sedan 0.6943, SUV 0.8366 — a 47% spread. None of the models above could see it, because vehicle_type is categorical and had never been encoded into the regression. That meant they returned the same cost for an SUV and a hatchback on an identical route, which is both wrong and obviously wrong to any user.

One-hot encoding it (Hatchback as baseline) gives the model that ships from Week 6 onward:

ModelTest R²Test MAE
Linear + interaction + vehicle one-hots0.999707₹39.66

Learned per-vehicle offsets, relative to Hatchback: SUV +₹129.63, Sedan +₹64.43 — the correct ordering, recovered from data.

Two things worth drawing out

  • Domain understanding beat model complexity. The win came from knowing what the number means, not from a bigger hypothesis class.
  • The model stays linear. Everything from Week 3 — the closed-form solution, the from-scratch gradient descent, the interpretable coefficients — still applies. A Random Forest would have cost all of that and performed worse.

Note for Part 12: this conclusion holds when distance and litres are given. Week 10 re-runs the same experiment when the model must infer distance from coordinates, and the result reverses. Knowing the boundary of your own finding is worth more than the finding.

Part 9 · Week 7Classification — the cost band, and what it is worth

The task asked for classification where it adds value. The interface needs one label that a number cannot supply, so that is the target: is this trip cheap or expensive for its length?

9.1 Why the target is ₹/km and not rupees

Absolute cost is close to determined by distance. A 1,200 km drive costs more than a 200 km one without being worse value, so classifying total cost would tell a user nothing they could not read off the headline figure.

cost_band is therefore a quartile split of ₹/km into Excellent / Good / Average / Poor. That isolates the thing the user is actually choosing between: vehicle, mileage and fuel.

9.2 The result, and the honest caveat

Both rows below are LogisticRegression on the canonical split, so they are directly comparable. Part 11 runs all five model families on both.

It earns its place because the interface needs the label, not because it demonstrates difficult learning. Volunteering this distinction is stronger than being caught by it.

9.3 The second row is the experiment

The two rows above are the same target, the same split and the same model. The only difference is that the second run drops toll_cost and fuel_consumption_litres — the two columns no traveller can supply, which the sub-models in Part 12 predict on their behalf.

So the 6.0-point gap is not a footnote about feature selection. It is a measurement of what those sub-models are worth, in the currency of the thing they feed. Without them the classifier still beats a 25.7% baseline comfortably; with them it does measurably better, and the difference is attributable rather than asserted.

This is also where the overfitting diagnosis finally bites

On the cost target, step 2 of the Task 5 checklist was close to uninformative: every model scored above R² 0.99 because the target is a formula, so train-versus-test told you almost nothing. Here it separates the models immediately.

A tree grown to purity memorises every training row and reports perfect training accuracy while losing 28 to 36 points on held-out data.

Note which model has the best raw test score. RandomForest edges LogisticRegression by 0.0018 — well inside the cross-validation spread — while carrying a train-test gap twenty times larger. The selection rule used throughout this project (error band, then the one-standard-error rule, then simplicity) picks the logistic model, and this is exactly the case it exists for: a score that is higher by less than its own uncertainty is not higher.

9.4 How the label earns its place in the product

A classifier that outputs one word is decoration unless the word changes a decision. This one sits on every result card, beside a figure the user is about to compare against two others.

Because the interface prices a hatchback, a sedan and an SUV on the same route, the three bands are read together rather than alone — and they separate cleanly. On Agra → Jaipur at 241 km:

The rupee figures alone would leave the user to work out whether ₹2,930 is bad value or simply a bigger car. The band answers that directly — and the second row shows the same three vehicles each moving up one band on a cheaper fuel, which is the comparison the whole section exists to make.

Part 10 · Week 8Why gradient descent stopped converging

10.1 The symptom

The Week 3 from-scratch gradient descent was re-run on the 25,000-row dataset with the Week 6 feature set. It behaved differently:

MeasureValue
R², sklearn vs gradient descent0.999707 vs 0.999701
Largest gap between the two models' predictions₹82.81
Largest gap between the two models' weights682.72
Epochs60,000 — did not early-stop

Predictions agree; weights do not converge. On the original 1,129 rows the largest weight gap was 0.042. Here it is 682.

10.2 The cause, measured

The Week 6 interaction term is fuel_bill = fuel_consumption_litres × fuel_price. That makes fuel_bill almost a deterministic function of fuel_consumption_litres:

MeasureValue
corr(litres, fuel_bill)0.989
Condition number of the Hessian, κ(H)≈ 16,000

Two near-collinear columns make the loss surface a long, narrow valley rather than a round bowl. Many different weight combinations fit almost equally well. Gradient descent descends into the valley quickly and then crawls along its nearly-flat floor; sklearn's normal equations jump straight to one specific point. Both are correct — they simply pick different points in the same valley, which is why the predictions match while the weights do not.

10.3 Ridge fixes the conditioning, and costs too much

Ridge regression adds an L2 penalty, which lifts the flat direction of the valley:

minimise   ||Xw − y||² + α||w||²     →     w = (XTX + αI)−1XTy

Ridge does exactly what the theory promises — κ falls ~50× and gradient descent converges essentially exactly — and it costs six times the error to do it.

The conclusion — a strong viva answer

We do not actually need gradient descent. The normal equations solve this model exactly, in one step, and are indifferent to the condition number. Gradient descent was an exercise in understanding what fit() does — and the exercise taught something more useful than “the two methods agree”:

An ill-conditioned problem is not a broken model. It is a problem where iterative solvers struggle and direct solvers do not. Reaching for Ridge here would trade real accuracy to fix a difficulty that only exists because of the solver we chose.

So the model that ships is ordinary least squares with the Week 6 interaction term, solved in closed form. Week 9 then confirms this mechanically: a GridSearchCV over α drives it to the bottom of the grid without being told any of the above.

Also measured: the divergence threshold for the learning rate on this data is lr ≈ 0.45 (0.5 blows up to inf), closely matching the ≈ 0.44 ceiling found on the original CSV. Training uses 0.4. All of this is surfaced in the application through the /api/loss-curve endpoint rather than hidden.
