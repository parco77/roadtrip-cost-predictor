Part 15Viva preparation — 60 questions with answers

How to use this part

The questions are ordered from most to least likely. Q1–Q12 are the ones that will almost certainly be asked — if time is short, learn those and the three formulas in Q13, Q21 and Q30.

Two habits matter more than any single answer. First, volunteer the honest number before being asked for it: say “R² 0.9645 for a real prediction, and 0.9997 if the model is handed its own inputs” rather than waiting to be caught. Second, when you do not know something, say what you would do to find out — that answers the question behind the question.

Group A — the questions you will definitely get

Q1. What is this project, in one minute?

It predicts the cost of a road trip between any two of 537 Indian cities. You give it two city names, a vehicle, a fuel type and a departure time; it returns a cost estimate with a breakdown.

It is a regression problem — the target total_trip_cost is continuous — with two supporting classification tasks. It began from a 1,140-row dataset over 9 fixed city pairs. Over ten weeks it grew into a chained pipeline of six sub-models: road distance, fuel efficiency, litres burnt, toll, parking and traffic level are each predicted, and a final regressor totals them. The honest accuracy is R² 0.9645, MAE ₹391.

Q2. Your R² is 0.9997. Isn't that suspiciously high?

Yes, and that is the most important thing I found. That 0.9997 was measured with the true toll_cost, parking_cost and fuel_consumption_litres handed to the model as input features. Those are three of the four terms of the cost formula, so the model was essentially being asked to add up numbers it had already been given.

It answers the question “given the litres burnt and the toll paid, can you total the bill?” A real user knows none of those before setting off. When the model has to derive them itself, the score is R² 0.9645, MAE ₹391 — and that is the figure the deployed API reports as served, with the larger one labelled fed_true_components beside it and the gap between them named.

Q3. Why is the error ₹391? That seems like a lot.

It is, and it is almost entirely the data's fault rather than the model's. I decomposed it:

  • toll noise: ± ₹219 — the generator sets toll = distance × N(1.301, 0.360), so the per-trip spread is random by construction
  • parking noise: ± ₹50 — drawn uniformly from a fixed set, independent of everything
  • fuel bill: ± ₹479 — this one inherits the distance prediction error

Added in quadrature that is ₹529, against a model RMSE of ₹596 — a ratio of 1.13. The two agree closely enough to say the chain is near the floor this dataset allows. Only the fuel-bill term could be reduced by a better model; the other two are irreducible.

Q4. Explain gradient descent, and show that you implemented it yourself.

Three steps, repeated. One: compute the loss — mean squared error, L = (1/n)∑(ŷ − y)². Two: compute the gradients, which point in the direction that increases the loss: ∂L/∂w = (2/n) Xᵀ·error and ∂L/∂b = (2/n)∑error. Three: step in the opposite direction — that is the “descent” — w ← w − lr·∂L/∂w.

I wrote it in pure NumPy, no sklearn, and all six gradients are computed in one matrix operation X.T @ error rather than a Python loop per feature. It stops when the loss changes by less than 1e−8. With learning rate 0.2 it converged at epoch 14,317 out of a 50,000 budget.

Q5. Did your from-scratch version match sklearn?

Yes, to within 0.03%. The largest disagreement on any parameter was 0.042 — four paise — against coefficients in the hundreds and thousands. Across all 1,129 trips the two models' predictions never differed by more than ₹0.011, and R² was identical to six decimal places (0.992841).

And they had to match. The MSE loss for linear regression is convex — a bowl with exactly one minimum. sklearn jumps to the bottom algebraically by solving the normal equations; gradient descent walks down to the same bottom. Given a sensible learning rate and enough epochs there is nowhere else for either to land.

Q6. What would make them not match?

Four things, and I tested each rather than just listing them:

  • Learning rate too small — at lr = 0.001 with a 3,000-epoch budget the weights were still about 2,025 away from sklearn's. The loss was still falling when the loop ended.
  • Too few epochs — at lr = 0.2 the gap collapses from ~1,260 at 100 epochs to 0.94 at 10,000 to effectively zero by 50,000.
  • Learning rate too large — above about lr = 0.44 on this data the updates overshoot the minimum, the loss oscillates and blows up to inf.
  • Unscaled features — features on very different ranges turn the loss surface into a narrow valley, and one learning rate cannot suit every weight.

Q7. Why did you drop the one-hot city columns in Week 3? That looks like you were avoiding a hard case.

It was necessary, not convenient. In the original dataset every route is a fixed pair — Delhi only ever goes to Jaipur. So start_city_Delhi and destination_city_Jaipur contain identical values in every row.

That makes the design matrix rank-deficient: infinitely many different weight combinations give the exact same predictions. sklearn silently picks one using the pseudo-inverse; gradient descent, starting from zeros, converges on a different one. Both are correct, and the two weight vectors would look completely unrelated.

Since the whole point of Week 3 is to compare the two parameter sets, keeping those columns would have made a correct result look broken. Later weeks encode vehicle_type as one-hots, where there is no such degeneracy — and Week 6 shows dropping that was a real bug.

Q8. How did you know the dataset was synthetic?

I tested it rather than guessing. I subtracted the cost components I could compute exactly — fuel, toll, parking — and looked at the residual. If it were noise it would scatter around zero.

Instead it was never negative, never zero, and correlated +0.85 with distance. Dividing by distance gave a per-kilometre rate, and grouping that rate by each categorical column showed only vehicle_type split it — cleanly: Hatchback 0.5694, Sedan 0.6943, SUV 0.8366 ₹/km. Fuel type, traffic and weather were all flat.

Reassembling the formula and evaluating it gives R² = 0.999162 from arithmetic alone, mean absolute error ₹18.44. That is not a model fit — it is the generating equation, recovered.

Q9. If the data is synthetic, what have you actually learned?

Three things, and I would give all three.

First, the discovery itself is a result. Recovering the generating formula to R² 0.999162 required a hypothesis, a method and a test.

Second, the geography is real. Cities and coordinates come from GeoNames, and I collected 840 actual road distances from the OSRM routing engine. The distance sub-model is trained on observed data and beats the hardcoded constant by 10.7% on MAE.

Third, it forced the project to be honest about which numbers mean what. Because I knew the target was a formula, I could recognise that a near-perfect R² was arithmetic rather than skill — which is exactly the reasoning that produced the two-number reporting in the API. A student who never noticed would have submitted R² 0.9997 and believed it.

Q10. Walk me through Task 5.

Six steps, applied to every model in the project, all scored through one shared module so the notebook, the report and the API cannot disagree.

  1. Metrics — RSS, RMSE, R² for regression; accuracy, precision, recall, F1 (macro-averaged) for classification.
  2. Overfit / underfit — train vs test with stated thresholds: a gap above 0.05 is overfitting, both below 0.50 is underfitting.
  3. Validation — 5-fold cross-validation on the training data, plus a 200-resample bootstrap.
  4. Comparison — one table per family, on one common split.
  5. Tuning — GridSearchCV over Ridge α, RandomizedSearchCV over RandomForest, GridSearchCV on the traffic classifier.
  6. Advanced models — RandomForest (bagging), AdaBoost and GradientBoosting, in every table.

Best regressor: LinearRegression, R² 0.999707, RMSE ₹54.16. Best classifiers: LogisticRegression on cost-band at 85.62%, and GradientBoosting on traffic at 56.96% against a 37.66% baseline.

Q11. Did hyperparameter tuning improve your model?

Two of the three searches improved, one did not — and the pattern is the point.

  • Ridge α improved, but only against sklearn's default α = 1.0, and it did so by driving the penalty to the bottom of the grid. The real finding is that this problem wants no regularisation.
  • RandomForest did not improve. Twelve random draws over four hyperparameters could not beat 300 trees at their defaults.
  • The traffic classifier improved genuinely, by +0.92 accuracy points, with max_depth=4.

The lesson: tuning pays where the data is noisy and the model can overfit it. Where the target is a formula and the features already span it, tuning has nothing to do — and a search reporting no improvement is evidence the model was specified correctly, not evidence the search was wasted.

Q12. Why grid-search Ridge if your best model is plain LinearRegression?

Because LinearRegression has no hyperparameter worth tuning — a grid search over it would be theatre. Ridge is the same model plus exactly one knob, so searching α is a real search whose answer means something.

And it had a specific hypothesis to test. In Week 8 I had rejected Ridge by hand, arguing from the Hessian condition number that regularisation fixes the conditioning at an unacceptable cost in accuracy. If that argument was right, the search should reach the same verdict mechanically without being told the theory. It did: the CV score is flat for every α ≤ 0.01 — where the penalty is too small to do anything, so Ridge is ordinary least squares — and falls monotonically above it, down to R² 0.835 at α = 1000.

Group B — concepts and definitions

Q13. Define RSS, RMSE, MAE and R², and say when each is appropriate.

RSS = ∑(y − ŷ)² — the raw quantity least squares minimises. It is a sum, so it grows with row count: comparable between models on the same split, meaningless across different splits.

RMSE = √(RSS/n) — reads in the target's units. Squaring inside means one large error counts as much as many small ones, so quote it when large errors are what hurt.

MAE = (1/n)∑|y − ŷ| — average error, no squaring. The number to tell a user.

R² = 1 − RSS/TSS — fraction of variance explained. Its weakness is exactly what this project hit: when the target is nearly deterministic, R² sits so close to 1 that differences that matter in rupees become invisible. In my regression table every model except AdaBoost scores R² > 0.998, yet the worst is seven times worse in RMSE. That is why I read my tables from the RMSE column.

Q14. What is overfitting? How do you detect it, and did you see it?

Overfitting is when a model learns the training data's noise rather than its structure — so it scores well on data it has seen and badly on data it has not. Detection: compare train and test scores; a large gap is the signature.

I saw it in three places, and where I didn't see it is equally instructive:

  • cost_band classifier: DecisionTree train 1.0000 vs test 0.7918 — a gap of 0.21. RandomForest 1.0000 vs 0.8354.
  • toll sub-model: DecisionTree train R² 0.9856 vs test 0.6109 — a gap of 0.37. This is the first regression target with real injected noise, and the tree fits it.
  • parking sub-model: DecisionTree train 0.9888 vs test −1.00 — pure memorisation of pure noise, the cleanest example in the project.
  • The cost target: DecisionTree reaches train R² = 1.000000 and still holds test 0.9986. Because the target is a formula, memorising and learning the rule give nearly the same predictions. So on that target the train/test check is close to uninformative — worth knowing, because it means a “good fit” verdict there is a property of the data, not proof of careful modelling.

Q15. What is underfitting? Show me an example from your project.

Underfitting is when the model is too simple, or the features carry too little signal, to capture the relationship — so it scores badly on both train and test.

My best example is on the original dataset. Predicting traffic_level from departure_hour, all five classifiers land on exactly 0.5088 — the majority-class baseline to four decimal places — with macro F1 of 0.2248 and a lift of +0.0 points. All five are flagged underfitting.

That is not five models failing. It is five models all discovering the same thing: in that data traffic is statistically independent of hour, so the best available strategy is to always predict the majority class. The macro F1 of 0.22 is the giveaway — predicting one class out of three scores about a third on macro recall however good the accuracy looks.

Q16. Explain cross-validation, and why the standard deviation matters more than the mean.

K-fold splits the training data into k parts, trains on k−1 and validates on the remaining one, k times. You get k scores.

The mean is a better estimate than a single split, because a single split's score depends on which rows happened to land in the test set. But the standard deviation across folds answers a different and often more useful question: is this model stable, or did it get lucky? A model with a high mean and a wide spread is unreliable — it is sensitive to which particular rows it saw.

In my project the spreads on 25,000 rows are 1e−5 to 4e−4 — so I say plainly that cross-validation confirmed stability but did not discriminate between models. It earns its place on the original 1,140-row dataset, where the same models and the same code give spreads 4 to 20 times wider. That contrast is the clearest argument for why cross-validation is on the checklist at all: with one split you would never see it.

Q17. What is the bootstrap, and how does it differ from k-fold?

Both estimate variability, but of different things. K-fold asks “how does the score vary across disjoint held-out folds?” Bootstrap asks “how would the score vary if I had drawn a different training sample of the same size?” — so it resamples the training rows with replacement, refits, and scores each fit on a fixed test set.

I ran 200 resamples on the best regressor: mean R² 0.999707, standard deviation 1e−6, 95% interval [0.999705, 0.999708] — a width of about 3e−6. The fit does not depend in any meaningful way on which rows were drawn.

Q18. Why macro-averaged precision and recall, not weighted?

Macro weights every class equally; weighted weights by class frequency. With an imbalanced target, weighted averaging can hide a class the model never predicts at all — you get a respectable score while one class is being completely ignored.

My project contains the proof. On the original dataset the traffic classifiers score 50.88% accuracy, which sounds passable. Their macro F1 is 0.2248, which immediately reveals they are predicting a single class out of three. Macro averaging is what makes that visible.

Q19. Why is one-hot encoding correct here and not label encoding?

Label encoding would map Hatchback→0, Sedan→1, SUV→2. That asserts an ordering and a spacing — that an SUV is “twice” a Sedan, and that Sedan sits exactly halfway between the other two. None of that is meaningful for a nominal category, and a linear model would take the arithmetic literally.

One-hot gives each category its own independent column and asserts no ordering. I use drop_first=True to avoid the dummy variable trap: if you keep all three vehicle columns they sum to 1 in every row, which is perfectly collinear with the intercept and makes the design matrix singular.

Label encoding would be right for a genuinely ordinal variable — and traffic_level (Low < Medium < High) is arguably one. I still used one-hot there for consistency, which is a defensible choice rather than the only one.

Q20. Why MinMaxScaler rather than StandardScaler?

MinMax maps to a bounded 0–1 range: (x − min)/(max − min). Standardisation subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, giving unbounded values centred on zero.

I chose MinMax for two reasons. It suits gradient descent — all partial derivatives land on a comparable scale, so a single learning rate works for every weight. And it makes the coefficients readable: because every feature spans exactly 0–1, a weight is literally “how many rupees the cost changes as this feature moves from its minimum to its maximum”, which is why I could immediately identify fuel_consumption_litres at +2938 as the dominant driver.

StandardScaler would be the better choice if the features were roughly Gaussian and I cared about outlier robustness — MinMax is sensitive to extreme values, which is part of why the outlier step comes first.

Q21. Why did you scale the features but not the target?

Deliberately, so the learned coefficients stay in rupees and remain interpretable. Scaling the target would leave the model equally accurate and completely unreadable — every weight would be in arbitrary units and I could not tell an examiner what any of them meant.

Scaling the target is worth doing when the optimiser struggles with the target's magnitude, or when comparing across differently-scaled targets. Neither applied here.

Q22. What is bagging? What is boosting? Which did better for you?

Bagging (RandomForest) trains many trees in parallel on bootstrap samples of the rows, each considering a random subset of features, then averages them. It attacks variance: individual deep trees overfit, and averaging many decorrelated ones cancels much of that out.

Boosting (AdaBoost, GradientBoosting) trains weak learners sequentially, each one focused on what the previous ones got wrong — AdaBoost by re-weighting misclassified rows, GradientBoosting by fitting the residuals. It attacks bias.

In my project RandomForest was the strongest ensemble on most targets and GradientBoosting won the traffic classification. AdaBoost was consistently the weakest, and I can say why: it fits shallow stumps and chases the rows it got wrong, which on a smooth additive target with injected noise means spending its capacity on the noisiest rows instead of representing the smooth part.

The headline, though, is that none of the three beat a correctly specified linear model on the cost target — ₹54 RMSE for linear against ₹87 for RandomForest and ₹399 for AdaBoost.

Q23. Why did a linear model beat a Random Forest? Isn't that unusual?

Not when the underlying relationship really is linear-plus-a-known-product, which is exactly this case. The cost is a sum of terms, one of which is a product of two features. Once I supply that product as a column, a linear model can represent the target exactly.

A tree ensemble has to approximate the same relationship with axis-aligned splits. It gets close, but it can never be exact, and it pays in variance for the flexibility it does not need.

The general principle: match the model to the structure of the problem. Trees win when the structure is something you cannot write down — which is precisely what happens in my end-to-end model, where they beat the linear one by a factor of seven because the missing structure is a map.

Q24. What is regularisation? Why did you reject it?

Regularisation adds a penalty on the size of the weights, trading a little bias for a reduction in variance. Ridge (L2) minimises ||Xw − y||² + α||w||², giving w = (XᵀX + αI)⁻&sup9;Xᵀy — and the αI term is exactly what makes the matrix better conditioned. Lasso (L1) penalises absolute values instead and can drive weights to exactly zero, so it also does feature selection.

I rejected it because I measured what it cost. Ridge did fix my conditioning problem — condition number from 16,155 down to 263 — and gradient descent then converged exactly. But test MAE went from ₹39.66 to ₹255.42, six times worse.

The reason is that I do not need gradient descent. The normal equations solve this model exactly in one step and are indifferent to the condition number. So regularising would trade real accuracy to fix a difficulty that only exists because of the solver I chose. Week 9's grid search confirmed it independently by driving α to the bottom of the grid.

Q25. What is multicollinearity? Did you have it?

Multicollinearity is when features are strongly linearly related to each other. The fit stays valid but the individual coefficients become unstable — many different weight combinations explain the data almost equally well, so the weights are no longer trustworthy as “the effect of this feature”.

I had it twice, deliberately and accidentally. Deliberately: my Week 6 interaction term fuel_bill = litres × price correlates 0.989 with litres, which gave a Hessian condition number of about 16,000 and is why gradient descent stopped converging on weights while still agreeing on predictions. Accidentally: the fixed-pair city columns in Week 2 were perfectly collinear — identical columns — which is why I dropped them.

I also dropped estimated_time_minutes for this reason: it is nearly a copy of distance_km and adds collinearity without adding information.

Q26. Explain the bias–variance tradeoff using your own results.

Bias is error from a model too simple to capture the truth; variance is error from a model so flexible it fits the noise. Total error is roughly bias² + variance + irreducible noise, and you usually trade one against the other.

My clearest example is the parking sub-model. The data has no signal — parking is drawn uniformly at random — so all the error is irreducible noise. A linear model scores R² ≈ 0.000 (high bias, no variance: it just predicts the mean). A DecisionTree scores train 0.9888 and test −1.00 — it drove bias to zero on the training set by memorising noise, and paid for it with enormous variance.

The traffic classifier shows the useful middle. An unrestricted forest memorised the training rows; capping max_depth at 4 added bias and reduced variance, and the test score went up by 0.92 points. That is the tradeoff being resolved in the right direction.

Q27. Why is a train/test split necessary at all? And why 80/20?

Because a model's score on data it was fitted to is not evidence it will work on data it has not seen — a model that memorises scores perfectly and generalises not at all. The test set simulates unseen data.

80/20 is a convention balancing two needs: enough training data to fit well, enough test data for the score to be stable. On 25,000 rows that gives 20,000 train and 5,000 test, which is comfortable on both counts. I fixed random_state=42 so the split is identical everywhere in the project — otherwise the model comparison tables would not be comparing like with like.

For the classifiers I use a stratified split, so each class appears in the same proportion in both halves. Without it a rare class could land almost entirely in one side.

Q28. What is data leakage? Did it happen to you?

Leakage is when information from outside the training data influences the model, making the evaluation optimistic. Yes, it happened, and I found and fixed it.

My original code fitted MinMaxScaler on the whole feature matrix before train_test_split. So the test set's minima and maxima influenced the transformation applied to the training data. The effect is small for MinMax on this data, but it is precisely the mistake cross-validation exists to catch.

The fix is to wrap every model in Pipeline([MinMaxScaler, model]). Inside a Pipeline, cross-validation re-fits the scaler on each training fold, so nothing from the validation fold ever reaches it.

There is a second, subtler kind I also handled: when chaining sub-models, predictions on their own training rows are optimistic. So the training component features come from cross_val_predict — out-of-fold — meaning no row's features were produced by a model that had already seen that row. That is stacking done correctly.

Q29. Why sine and cosine for the departure hour?

Because hour is cyclical. 23:00 and 00:00 are one hour apart, but as raw integers they are 23 apart — the maximum possible distance. Any model treating the number literally would think late night and early morning are opposite extremes.

Mapping to sin(2πh/24) and cos(2πh/24) places the hours on a circle, so 23:00 and 00:00 end up adjacent. I do the same for month, so December and January are neighbours.

I also add an explicit is_peak flag, because sin/cos alone cannot express “high between 8–11 and again between 17–21” — that is a two-humped shape a single sinusoid cannot produce, so a linear model is handed it directly.

Q30. Why is accuracy alone not a result?

Because it has no meaning without its baseline. If 90% of a dataset is one class, a model that always predicts that class scores 90% accuracy while having learned nothing.

So I always report the majority-class baseline beside the accuracy. My traffic classifier scores 56.96% against a 37.66% baseline — a lift of +19.3 points, which is a real if modest result. My original-data classifiers score 50.88% against a 50.88% baseline — a lift of exactly zero, which is no result at all. The two accuracies are similar; the two results could not be more different.

Group C — specifics of this project

Q31. What were the five constants you removed, and why did it matter?

WINDING_FACTOR = 1.2355 (haversine to road km), TOLL_RATE = 1.301 (₹/km), DEFAULT_PARKING = 70.0, TRAFFIC_MULT (fuel burn by traffic level), and MAINTENANCE (₹/km by vehicle).

It mattered because only the final addition was a model. Everything above it was arithmetic, and that arithmetic produced fuel_consumption_litres — the regressor's strongest feature at correlation +0.96. So the model was being handed three of the four terms of the cost formula. Each constant is now a fitted sub-model with its own measured error, and every table keeps the constant as its baseline row: a model that cannot beat the thing it replaced has not earned its place.

Q32. Your distance model only improves on the constant by 10.7%. Was it worth it?

Yes, and the honest framing is more interesting than the headline. The constant is nearly unbiased — averaged over 840 real routes it is within 0.4% of the truth. That is why it survived review for so long.

But averaged accuracy is not what a user experiences. Route by route the constant is off by 6.3% typically, and by up to 66% at the extremes. A user takes one trip, not the average of all of them, and on that trip the errors do not cancel.

Concretely: on Jaipur → Agra the constant predicts 274 km against a real 238 km — 15% high. On Surat → Bhavnagar it predicts 116 km against a real 339 km, because the Gulf of Khambhat is in the way. The model cannot fix all of that — endpoint coordinates only partly determine route shape — but 10.7% of MAE and 12% of RMSE is real, and it comes with a measured error bar rather than an assertion.

Q33. One of your sub-models failed completely. Why did you keep it in the report?

Because it is a result. I fitted six model families to parking_cost and every one scored test R² ≤ 0 — worse than simply predicting the mean.

That is the correct answer, not a bug. The generator draws parking uniformly from a fixed set, independently of every other column, so nothing in the data predicts it. A model that appeared to predict it would be evidence of leakage.

So the application serves the training mean and /api/metrics discloses the failure, with a test asserting the disclosure stays there. A model that correctly discovers a variable is unpredictable is worth more than one tuned until it looks respectable — and hiding it would have made the other five sub-models less believable, not more.

Q34. What is stacking, and why did you need it?

Stacking is layering models: the outputs of several base models become the input features of a final one. My chain is exactly that — six sub-models feeding a cost regressor.

It needs care for two reasons. First, the train/serve mismatch: if the final model is trained on true component values and then served predicted ones, it has learned to trust inputs that are exact and receives inputs that are not. Second, optimism: a sub-model's predictions on its own training rows are better than it will manage in production, so training the final model on those would make it over-trust its inputs.

I handled both. The training component features come from cross_val_predict — out-of-fold, so no row's features came from a model that had seen it. And I measured the mismatch rather than assuming: trained-on-truth scored MAE ₹390.56, chain-consistent scored ₹391.16. The mismatch cost essentially nothing here, because the sub-model errors are close to zero-mean so the linear coefficients barely change. I ship the chain-consistent version because it is the correct construction, but I report that the mismatch did not bite rather than claiming a win I did not get.

Q35. You said feature engineering beats model complexity, then showed the opposite. Which is it?

Both, in different regimes — and knowing where the boundary is was the point.

When distance and litres are given (Week 6), the only thing a linear model cannot express is the product litres × price. Handing it over takes MAE from ₹143.85 to ₹42.60 and beats both ensembles, which had the same information but had to approximate the product.

End-to-end (Week 10), the model must first infer road distance from four coordinates. The engineered columns save ₹67 of MAE; switching to 300 trees saves ₹474. The ensemble wins by a factor of seven.

The resolution: feature engineering wins when the missing structure is something you can write down. When the missing structure is a map — coastlines, mountains, the Gulf of Khambhat — no finite set of hand-made interaction terms captures it, and partitioning space is exactly what trees do.

Q36. Your feature importance shows vehicle type at under 0.1%. So it doesn't matter?

That is the trap, and no — it does matter. Week 4 recovered a real per-km maintenance rate that differs by vehicle: Hatchback 0.5694, Sedan 0.6943, SUV 0.8366 ₹/km. Week 6 showed that dropping those columns made the app quote an identical price for an SUV and a hatchback on the same route, which is a bug I have a regression test for.

The rate spread is about 0.27 ₹/km — roughly ₹108 on a 400 km trip. In the end-to-end model that signal sits underneath the toll and parking noise, which is hundreds of rupees. The importance is low because the noise is large, not because the effect is absent.

This is exactly why the application serves the chained pipeline: pin distance and litres down first, and the vehicle term becomes visible again. It is also a good general warning about impurity importance — it tells you what the model used, not what is true.

Q37. Why did you collect real data instead of just using the generated dataset?

Because a distance model trained on the generated dataset would be circular. That dataset sets distance_km = haversine × N(1.2355, 0.082), so a model fitted to it would just recover the constant 1.2355 plus irreducible noise — and I would be “proving” that a model beats a constant by fitting a model to data the constant generated.

So I fetched 840 real driving distances from OSRM over a stratified sample of city pairs. That is genuinely observed data, and it is the only observed data in the project. It is what let me discover that the constant is unbiased-but-wrong, and to find the Gulf of Khambhat routes.

I am also explicit about the consequence: I keep a second, deliberately circular dataset-convention distance model, used only inside the chained evaluation so the cost comparison is apples-to-apples against a dataset whose costs came from its own distance column. The real-data model is the one that ships.

Q38. Why does your app prefer live routing over your own model?

Because a real answer beats a predicted one whenever it is available. OSRM returns the actual road distance; my model estimates it with MAE 30.67 km. If routing is reachable, using it is strictly better.

The model is the fallback, and that is where it earns its place — the previous fallback was the constant, with an unmeasured error. Now the fallback has a known held-out error, and the response reports distance_source so a user can see which path was taken.

Q39. What does the winding factor of 3.60 mean? Isn't that an outlier you should have removed?

It is real, and removing it would have been the wrong call. Surat and Bhavnagar sit on opposite shores of the Gulf of Khambhat: 94 km of straight line, but you have to drive around the head of the gulf, which is 339 km of road.

That point, and the 1.04 factor of Moradabad → Chanduasi at the other extreme, are the entire argument for using a model instead of a constant — no single multiplier can serve both. Deleting them would have removed the evidence for my own conclusion.

It also explains which model won: a tree ensemble, because it can isolate a region of the map. A linear fit gets dragged by such points rather than representing them.

Q40. How do you choose between two models that score almost the same?

Three filters, in order, and it is written down so it is reproducible.

  1. Test-error band — keep models within 2% of the best RMSE (or 0.5 accuracy points). Crucially, closeness is judged on the error, never on R².
  2. One-standard-error rule — of those, keep every model whose CV mean is within one standard error (sd/√k) of the best. This is the standard rule from Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman §7.10: a difference smaller than the noise in the CV estimate is not a real difference.
  3. Simplicity — take the simplest survivor. Here that is not only Occam's razor: a linear model keeps my from-scratch gradient descent applicable, which no ensemble does.

I know the first filter matters because I got it wrong first. My original rule used “R² within 0.5% relative”, and near R² = 0.99 that band swallowed a model with 15% more RMSE — it picked a Ridge fit at 53.7 km over a random forest at 46.6 km.

Q41. Why does your cost-band classifier score 85% when the traffic one only scores 57%? Isn't the 85% the better model?

No — the 57% is the better result, and I would volunteer that.

cost_band is a quartile split of ₹/km, and my Week 6 regressor already predicts ₹/km to within ₹42. So the classifier is being asked to discretise a quantity that is already known accurately. Its 85% is high by construction; it earns its place because the interface needs the label, not because it demonstrates difficult learning.

traffic_level is genuinely non-trivial: departure hour only partly determines traffic, so the ceiling is low. 57% against a 37.66% baseline is a lift of +19.3 points on a hard problem. A classifier claiming 95% there would be evidence of leakage, not skill.

Q42. What does your confusion matrix tell you beyond the accuracy?

How the model fails, which accuracy cannot show. For the traffic classifier, errors are concentrated between adjacent levels — Low confused with Medium, Medium with High — and it is most accurate at the extremes.

That is the right failure mode for an ordered target: it has learned the rush-hour structure and is uncertain in the middle, which is where the data genuinely is ambiguous. A model that confused Low with High would be a much more worrying 57%.

On the cost-band matrix the same reading applies: Excellent and Poor are classified best (F1 0.895 and 0.919), the two middle bands worst (0.816 and 0.801), because the boundary between “Good” and “Average” is an arbitrary quartile cut.

Q43. Why 5-fold and not 10-fold, or leave-one-out?

It is a bias–variance–cost tradeoff on the estimate. More folds means more training data per fold, so less pessimistic bias, but more models to fit and a higher-variance estimate. Leave-one-out is the extreme: nearly unbiased, expensive, and high variance.

5-fold is the standard compromise and the checklist's default. With 20,000 training rows each fold still has 16,000 rows to train on, which is plenty — and my measured CV standard deviations of 1e−5 confirm the estimate is stable, so more folds would buy nothing here. Fitting 7 models × 5 folds × several targets was already the bulk of my compute time.

Q44. What is the difference between GridSearchCV and RandomizedSearchCV, and when do you use each?

Grid search tries every combination in a specified grid — exhaustive and guaranteed to find the grid's best, but the cost is the product of all the axes. Randomized search samples a fixed number of random combinations from distributions — it covers a larger space for a fixed budget, and it does not waste effort on axes that turn out not to matter.

I used both, matched to the problem. Grid for Ridge α (one axis, 8 values — exhaustive is trivial and I wanted the whole curve to inspect). Grid for the traffic classifier (3 axes, 18 combinations — still cheap). Randomized for RandomForest, because the space is 4 axes with 4×4×4×3 = 192 combinations and 12 well-chosen draws tell me what I need for a sixteenth of the compute.

Q45. How long does everything take to run, and what is the biggest cost?

The OSRM data collection is the wall-clock bottleneck at about 6 minutes for 840 requests, rate-limited to 0.4 s apart to be a polite client on a free public server. It writes each row to disk as it goes, so a rate-limited run can resume.

Training the six sub-models takes about 4–5 minutes, the Task 5 sweep about 323 seconds, and the end-to-end benchmark about 2 minutes. The most expensive single step is the RandomizedSearchCV: 12 candidates × 5 folds of a 300-tree forest on 20,000 rows, about 86 seconds even parallelised across 20 cores.

Group D — harder and follow-up questions

Q46. Your model was trained on generated data. How would it perform on real trips?

Worse, and I can be specific about where. The cost structure should transfer reasonably — fuel = litres × price is physics, not an artefact. The distance model should transfer well, because it is trained on real OSRM data.

Three things would degrade. The traffic accuracy would almost certainly fall, because my 57% measures an injected rush-hour profile rather than real Indian roads — I say so explicitly. Toll would change most: real Indian tolls are per-plaza, not a smooth per-km rate, so the linear form is wrong even though it fits the generated data. Parking is currently unpredictable by construction; in reality it correlates with city size and destination type, so it would actually become more predictable with real data.

The honest summary: this project's methodology would transfer; its coefficients would need refitting; and its reported accuracy is an upper bound.

Q47. What would you do differently if you started again?

Four things. Test the data-generating process first — I reached Week 4 before asking whether the data was synthetic, and that question should have come in Week 1 when I noticed zero missing values and zero duplicates in 1,140 rows.

Split before any data-dependent transformation. My Week 2 outlier removal and scaling both happen before the split. I fixed the scaling with Pipelines; the outlier filter is still applied to the whole dataset, which is a real if minor leak.

Collect real data earlier. The 840 OSRM distances turned out to be the most valuable data in the project and they took six minutes to gather.

Report the served metric from the start. The ₹39.66 error bar sat next to predictions for weeks before I noticed no real prediction could achieve it.

Q48. How would you improve the accuracy from here?

The error budget answers this precisely, which is why I built it. Of the ₹596 RMSE, toll (±₹219) and parking (±₹50) are irreducible — drawn from random distributions by construction. No model can improve them.

The only improvable term is the fuel bill (±₹479), and it is improvable because it inherits the distance error. So the highest-value work is a better distance model: more real OSRM routes (840 is a small training set for a geographic problem), terrain and road-class features rather than just endpoint coordinates, or simply caching real routing more aggressively so the model is needed less often.

Everything else — a bigger ensemble, more tuning, more features on the cost regressor — would be working on the ₹67 that is left, and my RandomizedSearchCV already showed there is nothing there.

Q49. Why is there no neural network?

Because nothing in this problem calls for one, and I can say what would.

The cost target is a linear combination of terms plus one known product. A linear model with the right features fits it to R² 0.9997 and I can read every coefficient. A neural network would be slower to train, need far more data to justify its parameter count, lose the interpretability that makes the coefficients meaningful, and — on the evidence of the ensembles — almost certainly score worse.

Where one would help is the end-to-end problem: predicting cost from raw coordinates is a genuinely non-linear geographic function, and a network could learn a spatial embedding that trees approximate with axis-aligned splits. With substantially more real routing data, that would be the natural next experiment.

Q50. Your test set is 5,000 rows from the same generated distribution. Is that a fair test?

It is a fair test of generalisation within that distribution, and nothing more — which is the right way to state it. It answers “does the model work on trips it has not seen?” but not “does the model work on trips from a different distribution?”

A stronger design would hold out entire routes or entire regions, not random rows. Because I sample 5 trips per route, the same city pair can appear in both halves — so the model may benefit from having seen that route's geography. For the cost regressor this matters little (cost is a function of the numeric features, not of route identity), but for the distance model it would matter a lot, which is why that model is trained and tested on a separate real dataset with its own split.

Route-level or region-level cross-validation is the improvement I would make.

Q51. What happens if a user asks for a 3,000 km trip?

The prediction is returned with an explicit warning that it is extrapolating. The models were trained on 50–1,500 km, and beyond that range they have no evidence.

I chose to warn rather than refuse because a refusal is less useful than a caveated number, and less honest than silently guessing — which is the third option and the worst one. The warning names the trained range so the user can judge how far outside it they are.

Q52. Why does the same route cost differently at 3am and 9am?

Through traffic, and only through traffic. The traffic classifier infers the likely level from the departure hour; a higher traffic level multiplies fuel consumption (Low 0.944, Medium 1.045, High 1.165); more litres means a bigger fuel bill.

Distance, toll and parking are unaffected by departure time, which the API states in its response note. On Jaipur → Agra the full 24-hour spread is about ₹177. The departure-sweep endpoint charts this, using the classifier's probabilities rather than its most likely label — taking the argmax would collapse 24 hours onto 3 distinct values.

Q53. You have two accuracy numbers in your API. Isn't that confusing for a user?

The API leads with one and labels it served: R² 0.9645, MAE ₹391. That is the number a user sees beside their estimate. The larger figure is available as fed_true_components with a note saying it answers a different question, plus an explicit honesty_gap field.

Reporting only the flattering number would be misleading; reporting only the honest one would throw away a genuine result about the model and the dataset. Labelling both, with the gap named, is the version that is true and complete — and there is a regression test asserting the served figure is the smaller of the two, so the flattering number cannot creep back into the headline.

Q54. Why did you write the report generator instead of typing the numbers?

Because the report quotes roughly two hundred numbers. Typed by hand it would start with at least one transcription error and go stale the first time I retrained anything — and a report whose numbers disagree with the code is worse than no report.

So TASK5.md and the results sections of this document are generated from the same saved JSON that the notebook and the API read. Regenerating after a retrain is one command, and the four artefacts cannot contradict each other.

Q55. How do you know your results are reproducible?

Every random operation is seeded: random_state=42 for splits and models, a fixed seed for the route sampling and the bootstrap. I verified it in practice — after re-running the whole pipeline to fix the model file sizes, every metric came back bit-identical (R² 0.964542, MAE ₹391.16, distance MAE 30.67 km), which is why the notebook outputs and the generated report stayed valid without re-execution.

Beyond seeding: the build order is documented, each script says what it needs and what it produces, the 65 tests pin the behaviour, and the notebook chapters are generated by scripts so they can be rebuilt rather than hand-maintained.

Q56. What is the single most interesting thing you learned?

That a near-perfect score can be a statement about the question, not the model. R² 0.9997 was not a mistake or a bug — it was a correct measurement of something I had not intended to measure. The model really could total a bill given three of its four terms; it just could not do the thing a user needed.

Finding that required knowing where the data came from, which is why Week 4 mattered so much. It changed how I report every number in the project, and it is the reason I now instinctively ask “what exactly is this model being given?” before believing a score.

Q57. Which part of this project are you least confident about?

The toll sub-model. It does not beat the constant it replaced (R² 0.7949 against 0.7950), and I keep it mainly because it is fitted and measured rather than asserted. On the generated data that is defensible, since the per-trip spread is injected noise. But real Indian tolls are charged per plaza, not as a smooth per-kilometre rate, so I suspect the whole functional form is wrong and only looks acceptable because the synthetic data was generated that way. With real toll data I would expect to need a different model entirely — probably one keyed on which national highways a route uses.

Q58. If I gave you one more week, what would you do?

Collect more real routing data — several thousand OSRM routes instead of 840 — and add road-class and terrain features to the distance model, because the error budget says that is the only term worth improving. Then switch to route-level cross-validation so no city pair appears in both halves of the split, which is the weakest part of my current evaluation.

After that, replace the per-km toll model with something keyed on the highways a route actually uses, and re-run the whole Task 5 sweep to see which conclusions survive real data. I would expect the traffic accuracy to fall and the parking model to become viable.

Q59. Explain your project to someone who knows no machine learning.

“You tell it where you are driving from and to, what car you have, and when you are leaving. It tells you roughly what the trip will cost, and it shows its working — how much is fuel, how much is tolls, how much is wear on the car.”

“It learned the relationship from 25,000 example trips. The interesting part is that it is honest about what it does not know: it says the estimate is give-or-take about ₹400, and most of that is tolls and parking, which nobody can predict in advance for a specific trip.”

Q60. Anything you want to add?

One thing: the project contains as many negative results as positive ones, and they were kept deliberately. A parking model that could not beat the mean. A tuning search that found no improvement. A distance model that only improved things by 10.7%. A train/serve mismatch that turned out not to matter. A finding from Week 6 that reversed in Week 10.

Each of those was easier to delete than to explain. Keeping them is what makes the positive results believable — and every one of them taught me more than the successes did.
