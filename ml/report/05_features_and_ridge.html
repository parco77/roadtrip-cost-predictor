Part 8Feature engineering — the interaction terms

8.1 The testable claim

Part 7 named the linear model's structural blind spot:

The real fuel bill is the product litres × price, and a linear model can only add its features together, never multiply them.

That is a claim that can be tested. If the missing product is really the bottleneck, then supplying it as a feature should beat throwing a nonlinear model at the problem — because a tree ensemble has to approximate a product it can never represent exactly, whereas we can simply hand it over.

8.2 The experiment

Four models, same 25,000 rows, same 80/20 split, same six base features (distance_km, mileage, fuel_price, toll_cost, parking_cost, fuel_consumption_litres):

ModelTest R²Test MAE
Linear (no interaction)0.994666₹143.85
RandomForest, 300 trees0.998543₹69.97
GradientBoosting0.998560₹73.50
Linear + litres × price0.999614₹42.60

The prediction holds. One engineered column takes MAE from ₹143.85 to ₹42.60 — a 3.4× improvement — and beats both ensembles, which had access to exactly the same information but had to learn the product implicitly.

8.3 The second missing feature

Part 5 found the per-km maintenance rate splits by vehicle: Hatchback 0.5694, Sedan 0.6943, SUV 0.8366 — a 47% spread. None of the models above could see it, because vehicle_type is categorical and had never been encoded into the regression. That meant they returned the same cost for an SUV and a hatchback on an identical route, which is both wrong and obviously wrong to any user.

One-hot encoding it (Hatchback as baseline) recovers the correct ordering: learned offsets of SUV +₹129.63 and Sedan +₹64.43 relative to a hatchback.

8.4 The one-hots are not enough, and the reason is the same one

Those offsets are flat. The model applies the same ₹129.63 to an SUV whether the drive is 200 km or 1,200 km — but running costs differ per kilometre, so the real gap runs from roughly ₹50 to ₹320 across that range.

That error is invisible while the interface shows one vehicle at a time. It becomes the headline the moment it shows three side by side, which is what this product does. The fix is the same move as fuel_bill: a linear model cannot multiply unless you hand it the product, so distance × SUV and distance × Sedan go in as columns.

Feature setTest R²Test MAE
No interactions at all0.994795₹139.75
Without litres × price0.994918₹139.01
Without distance × vehicle0.999707₹39.66
Both interactions — the model that ships0.999778₹33.86

The first interaction is worth ₹105 of MAE, the second ₹6. The second looks small in aggregate and is not small where it matters: it is the entire difference between three vehicle prices that separate correctly with distance and three that do not. Part 10.7 measures that directly.

Two things worth drawing out

  • Domain understanding beat model complexity. The win came from knowing what the number means, not from a bigger hypothesis class.
  • The model stays linear. Everything from Part 7 — the closed-form solution, the from-scratch gradient descent, the interpretable coefficients — still applies. A Random Forest would have cost all of that and performed worse.

Note for Part 10: this conclusion holds when distance and litres are given. Part 10 re-runs the same experiment when the model must infer distance from coordinates, and the result reverses. Knowing the boundary of your own finding is worth more than the finding.

Part 9Regularisation and ill-conditioning

9.1 The symptom

The Part 7 from-scratch gradient descent was re-run on the 25,000-row dataset with the Part 8 feature set. It behaved differently:

MeasureValue
R², sklearn vs gradient descent0.999778 vs 0.999772
Largest gap between the two models' predictions₹83.95
Largest gap between the two models' weights722.59
Epochs60,000 — did not early-stop

Predictions agree; weights do not converge. On the original 1,129 rows the largest weight gap was 0.042. Here it is 682.

9.2 The cause, measured

The Part 8 interaction term is fuel_bill = fuel_consumption_litres × fuel_price. That makes fuel_bill almost a deterministic function of fuel_consumption_litres:

MeasureValue
corr(litres, fuel_bill)0.989
Condition number of the Hessian, κ(H)≈ 16,000

Two near-collinear columns make the loss surface a long, narrow valley rather than a round bowl. Many different weight combinations fit almost equally well. Gradient descent descends into the valley quickly and then crawls along its nearly-flat floor; sklearn's normal equations jump straight to one specific point. Both are correct — they simply pick different points in the same valley, which is why the predictions match while the weights do not.

9.3 Ridge fixes the conditioning, and costs too much

The conclusion — a strong viva answer

We do not actually need gradient descent. The normal equations solve this model exactly, in one step, and are indifferent to the condition number. Gradient descent was an exercise in understanding what fit() does — and the exercise taught something more useful than “the two methods agree”:

An ill-conditioned problem is not a broken model. It is a problem where iterative solvers struggle and direct solvers do not. Reaching for Ridge here would trade real accuracy to fix a difficulty that only exists because of the solver we chose.

So the model that ships is ordinary least squares with the Part 8 interaction term, solved in closed form. Part 12 then confirms this mechanically: a GridSearchCV over α drives it to the bottom of the grid without being told any of the above.

Also measured: the divergence threshold for the learning rate on this data is lr ≈ 0.45 (0.5 blows up to inf), closely matching the ≈ 0.44 ceiling found on the original CSV. Training uses 0.4. All of this is surfaced in the application through the /api/loss-curve endpoint rather than hidden.
